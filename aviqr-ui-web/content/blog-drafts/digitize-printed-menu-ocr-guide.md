# How to Digitize a Printed Restaurant Menu Without Retyping It

Most restaurants that put off switching to a QR menu aren't skeptical of the idea — they're
dreading the setup. A menu with 80 items, each needing a name, description, price, and
category typed in by hand, is a genuinely tedious afternoon (or several). That dread is
usually the actual blocker, more than any doubt about whether QR ordering works.

It doesn't have to be manual. If you already have a printed menu, a laminated card, or even
just a PDF, the fastest path is OCR — photograph or scan what you already have, and let
software read it and build the digital version, instead of typing every line yourself.

## What the OCR process actually looks like

1. **Photograph or upload your existing menu.** A clear phone photo of each page works —
   this doesn't need a professional scan.
2. **The software reads item names, descriptions, and prices off the image** and builds a
   structured menu automatically, matching items to categories where it can.
3. **You review and correct, not build from scratch.** OCR won't be perfect on every item —
   handwriting, unusual fonts, or a smudged price can trip it up — but reviewing and fixing
   a handful of entries takes minutes, not the hours full manual entry would.
4. **Add photos per item, if you have them.** This step is separate from OCR and genuinely
   does take manual effort, but it's optional — a menu without photos still works, it's just
   less persuasive to a browsing customer.

## What actually determines how fast this goes

- **Menu size** — a 20-item stall menu is realistically a 5-minute job; an 80-item multi-page
  restaurant menu with a lot of descriptive text will take longer, mostly at the review step.
- **How clean the source is** — a professionally printed menu with clear text OCRs cleanly; a
  handwritten specials board won't, and is faster to just type directly.
- **Whether prices are consistent** — menus with per-size or per-portion pricing (small/
  medium/large, half/full) need more review than flat single-price items, since OCR has to
  correctly associate each price with its variant.

## What to check before assuming a platform actually does this well

- **Does OCR extract prices and categories, or just item names?** Some tools only lift the
  text and still leave you to manually assign every price and category — which defeats most
  of the time savings.
- **Can you review and edit before it goes live**, or does it publish directly from the scan
  with no correction step? Unreviewed OCR output on a live customer-facing menu is a real risk
  — a misread price is the kind of mistake customers notice immediately.
- **Does it handle multi-column or multi-page menus**, or only a single simple list? A lot of
  real restaurant menus (starters/mains/desserts across several columns) trip up simpler tools.

## The short version

If a printed or PDF menu already exists, digitizing it shouldn't mean retyping it. OCR that
actually extracts item names, prices, and categories — with a review step before anything
goes live — turns a dreaded afternoon of data entry into a five-to-fifteen-minute task.
